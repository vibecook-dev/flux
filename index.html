Home media, rewritten.

flux is a media server for the films and shows you own. It scans a whole library in seconds, plays the moment you press play, reaches all your devices through your own Tailscale network, and works hand in hand with the AI agent you already use.

Anywhere

All it takes is a free Tailscale account.

No port forwarding, no reverse proxy, no certificates to renew and no new passwords. flux joins your private tailnet, and the Tailscale login you already have is how you sign in, from the sofa, a hotel room or a train.

Six things to set up, keep updated and keep secure, and your server is on the open internet.

  1. 01

    Get Tailscale

    Install it on the computer with your films and on your devices. The Personal plan is free, for up to six people.

  2. 02

    Switch on remote access

    One switch in flux’s settings, or --tailnet system. flux uses this computer’s Tailscale and answers on your tailnet, never the open internet.

  3. 03

    Open it anywhere

    At https://machine.tailnet.ts.net. Switch on HTTPS and flux uses your tailnet’s own certificate, so even the GPU lenses work on your phone.

Your tailnet is the guest list.

Family and friends sign in with their own Tailscale accounts, never a flux password. Your login is the Owner. Everyone else can watch, and the people you trust with the library get Admin, with one grant in your tailnet policy.

"grants": [{

  "dst": ["*"],

It skips work

It reads container indexes instead of decoding, and a rescan that finds nothing new opens no files and writes nothing.

No ffmpeg in the hot path

Headers are probed and video is remuxed inside flux. ffmpeg starts only when something must be transcoded.

Every segment stands alone

A video segment is a pure function of the file, the recipe and its number, so a seek anywhere is one plain request.

One small binary

A single Rust program: memory-safe, no runtime to install, no garbage collector pausing your film.

The app keeps up, too.

Your library is drawn on the GPU with WebGPU. Zooming through 10,000 posters on the Wall takes 3.6 ms of main-thread time a frame, well inside the 8.3 ms a 120 Hz display allows, and drops no frames. The first load is 110 kB, compressed.

03.6 ms8.3 ms · 120 Hz
How we measured, and what it doesn’t say
  • One M1 Max (64 GB) on macOS, with both servers at the same low priority, network metadata off, background jobs and folder watching off. Timed over HTTP on loopback.
  • A synthetic library: 7 short seed clips cloned into 14,760 media files under messy names, plus 1,911 sidecar files. Playback used real encodes.
  • The servers do different work by design: Jellyfin runs ffprobe on every file and reads NFOs and artwork; flux probes headers in-process and reads neither.
  • Small samples: 3 full-library runs for flux and 2 for Jellyfin, and Jellyfin’s cold scan has taken 900–1,063 s across sessions on this machine.
  • Not every result is a big win: a TrueHD 7.1 file that needs an audio transcode starts 3.5× sooner, and the first play of a long MPEG-TS only 1.5× sooner.
  • Nothing here was measured on Linux, a NAS or a spinning disk, and Plex wasn’t measured at all.

Agent-native

Your agent knows more than any database.

It opens one contract to the agent you already use, Claude Code, Codex or anything that speaks MCP, so it can identify rare films, write descriptions, find posters and tidy your files from what it finds on the open web.
